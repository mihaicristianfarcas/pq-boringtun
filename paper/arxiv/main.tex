\begin{abstract}
WireGuard's key exchange relies entirely on X25519, which Shor's algorithm will break once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists; under the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat model, traffic recorded today is already at risk. With ML-KEM standardized as FIPS~203 in August 2024, the question for deployed VPNs is no longer which post-quantum primitive to adopt but what adopting one actually costs. This paper answers that question for WireGuard by integrating ML-KEM-768 alongside X25519 into the Noise IK handshake of BoringTun, Cloudflare's production Rust userspace WireGuard, following the established concatenate-then-KDF hybrid pattern. The intervention is deliberately minimal: roughly 800 added lines behind a Cargo feature flag, about a quarter of them tests, with the data-transport path untouched and no kernel modifications required. A simpler baseline that injects ML-KEM-derived keying material into WireGuard's existing preshared-key (PSK) slot is also implemented as a lower bound on engineering effort. Both approaches are evaluated on three platforms (Apple M4, Raspberry Pi 5, AMD Ryzen 7 5700X), measuring handshake latency, transport throughput, primitive-level microbenchmarks, MTU behaviour, RTT/loss tolerance, per-peer memory footprint, and denial-of-service asymmetry under a saturating handshake flood. The measured cost is concentrated on the wire rather than in the CPU: handshake cryptography stays under half a millisecond of added latency even on the Raspberry Pi, throughput is unchanged, per-peer memory grows by a fixed 6.4~KB, and WireGuard's MAC1 flood defence carries over intact, while the handshake grows by 2272 bytes and survives IP-layer fragmentation across the measured MTU range. The construction is secure as long as at least one of X25519 or ML-KEM remains unbroken, and provides per-session forward secrecy that the PSK-slot approach cannot match. The implementation is further validated end-to-end over the public internet, establishing hybrid tunnels between Romania and a US-East cloud host and round-tripping integrity-checked payloads through them. To our knowledge, this is the first FIPS~203-compliant hybrid WireGuard implementation built on a production Rust codebase.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Post-Quantum Cryptography, WireGuard, ML-KEM, FIPS 203, Hybrid Key Exchange, Noise Protocol, Rust, BoringTun.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Public-key cryptography underpins virtually all secure communication on the internet: VPN tunnels, TLS handshakes, SSH connections all depend on the computational hardness of problems like integer factorization and the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm. This assumption has held for decades, but will not hold forever. Shor's polynomial-time quantum algorithm~\cite{shor1994algorithms} solves both problems, so a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer would break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. Current estimates put the cost of factoring RSA-2048 at roughly millions of physical qubits with today's error-correction techniques~\cite{gidney2021factor}, and hardware roadmaps from IBM, Google, and others suggest that scale could be reached within the next decade or two.

In response, NIST launched its Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) Standardization Project in 2016 and finalized the first round of standards in August 2024: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, key encapsulation)~\cite{fips203}, FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, digital signatures), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, hash-based signatures). For VPN key exchange, ML-KEM is the directly relevant standard. NIST has also set a hard timeline: classical key exchange algorithms are to be deprecated by 2030 and disallowed entirely by 2035~\cite{nistir8547}.

WireGuard~\cite{donenfeld2017wireguard} is among the most important migration targets. Since 2017 it has largely replaced IPsec and OpenVPN as the go-to VPN protocol, thanks to its small codebase, strong performance, and easy auditability. It ships in the Linux kernel, runs on every major operating system, and is deployed at scale by Cloudflare, Mullvad, NordVPN, Tailscale, and many enterprises. WireGuard's cryptographic design is deliberately opinionated, however: a fixed cipher suite with no negotiation. This rigidity is a security advantage (cipher agility has been a recurring source of vulnerabilities in TLS and IPsec), but it makes migration harder, since there is no built-in way to swap in a different key exchange algorithm.

WireGuard does provide two natural handles for post-quantum augmentation. The first is the preshared-key (PSK) slot, a 256-bit symmetric key that is mixed into the handshake's key derivation chain. If a post-quantum KEM is used out-of-band to establish this PSK, the resulting handshake is quantum-resistant against passive adversaries, at the cost of requiring separate key distribution infrastructure. The second approach is to modify the Noise IK handshake itself, embedding ML-KEM operations alongside the existing X25519 exchange so that both shared secrets contribute to the final session key in every handshake. The latter is what we call the \emph{full hybrid} approach.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
  \item A \textbf{full hybrid handshake} in a production codebase: a modified BoringTun that runs both X25519 and ML-KEM-768 in every handshake, mixing both shared secrets into the Noise chaining key via HMAC-BLAKE2s. The change is deliberately small --- roughly 800 added lines across seven files, about a quarter of them tests, all behind a Cargo feature flag --- and leaves the data-transport phase untouched.
  \item A \textbf{PSK-slot baseline}: a small CLI tool that performs ML-KEM-768 key encapsulation between two peers out-of-band and feeds the resulting 256-bit key into BoringTun's \texttt{preshared\_key} field, as a lower bound on engineering effort.
  \item A \textbf{three-platform empirical evaluation} on Apple M4, Raspberry Pi 5, and AMD Ryzen 7 5700X, covering handshake latency, transport throughput, primitive-level microbenchmarks, an ML-KEM parameter-set sweep, a path-MTU sweep, an RTT$\times$loss matrix, per-peer memory footprint, and DoS asymmetry under a saturating handshake flood --- complemented by an end-to-end validation over a real transcontinental internet path.
  \item An \textbf{informal security argument} comparing the two approaches, with a discussion of where each one fits in practice and what is required to extend authentication to the post-quantum setting.
\end{enumerate}

The novelty here is one of engineering and evidence rather than cryptographic construction. The hybrid follows the well-understood concatenate-then-KDF combiner, and the goal is not the ultimate production-ready post-quantum VPN --- formally verified protocol designs exist for that ambition~\cite{lafourcade2025hybrid,hashimoto2026rkem}. The question addressed here is a complementary and, for operators, more immediate one: how invasive is it, really, to put standardized ML-KEM into a deployed WireGuard implementation, and what does it cost across hardware tiers, network conditions, and adversarial load? The answer --- a few hundred lines of feature-gated Rust whose overhead is dominated by handshake-message size rather than computation --- is the paper's central result. The security argument is correspondingly informal; a formal proof in the eCK-PFS-PSK model is left as future work.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The quantum threat and HNDL}

A quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could derive an X25519 private key from the corresponding public key and reconstruct any session key derived from it. What makes this urgent before such a machine exists is the \emph{harvest-now-decrypt-later} (HNDL) attack model: an adversary passively records encrypted traffic today --- WireGuard handshakes, transport data, all of it --- and decrypts it once a quantum computer becomes available. Traffic encrypted today with classical-only key exchange may therefore already be compromised in a meaningful sense.

Symmetric cryptography is largely safe: Grover's algorithm provides only a quadratic speedup, effectively halving key lengths, so AES-256, ChaCha20-Poly1305, BLAKE2s, and HMAC-based key derivation remain unaffected. The migration problem is confined to the asymmetric key exchange layer.

\subsection{WireGuard and the PSK slot}

WireGuard~\cite{donenfeld2017wireguard} is a layer-3 VPN protocol built around a fixed set of modern primitives and a deliberately minimal state machine. The handshake uses the Noise Protocol Framework~\cite{noise-protocol} (the IK pattern) with X25519 for Diffie-Hellman, HKDF~\cite{rfc5869} for key derivation, ChaCha20-Poly1305 for authenticated encryption, and BLAKE2s for hashing. The handshake produces a 1-RTT session: the initiator sends one message that authenticates its identity and commits an ephemeral key; the responder verifies and replies; both sides derive matching session keys. Sessions rekey after 120 seconds or $2^{60}$ messages, and all ephemeral keys are zeroed after use.

WireGuard includes an optional 256-bit preshared key (PSK), added specifically as a transitional post-quantum measure: when configured, it is mixed into the handshake's key derivation chain, so an attacker who breaks X25519 but never obtained the PSK still cannot decrypt the traffic. Its limitations are structural: the PSK is a long-lived static value, not an ephemeral secret, so it provides no forward secrecy for the quantum-resistant component, and its distribution and rotation require an out-of-band channel.

\subsection{Noise and the KEM/DH asymmetry}

Noise patterns specify the handshake messages and the DH operations performed at each step; a chaining key $\mathit{ck}$ and handshake hash $h$ absorb every DH result, public key, and encrypted payload via HMAC and BLAKE2s, binding all messages into one transcript. The main difficulty in migrating Noise to PQC is that DH is non-interactive --- either party computes the shared secret from the other's public key --- whereas a KEM is directional: one party encapsulates a secret to a specific public key, and only the holder of the corresponding private key can decapsulate it. The handshake flow therefore needs reworking when KEMs are introduced.

\subsection{ML-KEM (FIPS 203)}

ML-KEM was standardized as FIPS 203 in August 2024~\cite{fips203}. It is a key encapsulation mechanism derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber~\cite{bos2018crystals}, whose security rests on the Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) problem~\cite{langlois2015worst}, exposing the usual three operations (KeyGen, Encaps, Decaps). FIPS 203 specifies three parameter sets --- ML-KEM-512, -768, and -1024 (NIST categories 1, 3, 5) --- with encapsulation-key/ciphertext sizes of 800/768~B, 1184/1088~B, and 1568/1568~B respectively, all producing a 32-byte shared secret; NIST recommends ML-KEM-768 as the default. Thanks to the Number-Theoretic Transform, ML-KEM is computationally cheap on commodity CPUs --- often competitive with or faster than elliptic-curve operations --- but its keys and ciphertexts are 30--50$\times$ larger.

\subsection{Hybrid key exchange and concatenate-then-KDF}

Hybrid key exchange combines two key agreement algorithms so the resulting key is secure as long as at least one component holds --- prudent because ML-KEM, though standardized, has seen far less cryptanalytic scrutiny than X25519. The standard construction is the \emph{concatenate-then-KDF} pattern, formalized in NIST SP 800-56C Rev.~2~\cite{nist-sp800-56c}: the component shared secrets are concatenated, $Z' = Z \| T$, and fed into a single key derivation function. Giacon, Heuer, and Poettering~\cite{giacon2018combiners} prove the resulting KEM secure as long as either component is secure. The same pattern is used in hybrid TLS 1.3 deployments by Cloudflare and Google and in the IETF hybrid design draft~\cite{ietf-tls-hybrid-design}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{PQ-WireGuard (H\"ulsing et al., 2021).}
The most directly relevant prior work~\cite{hulsing2021pqwireguard} replaces WireGuard's X25519 handshake entirely with a KEM-based construction (Classic McEliece for authentication, Saber for ephemeral exchange), proved secure in the eCK-PFS-PSK model and verified in Tamarin. This paper differs in three ways: it is hybrid rather than purely post-quantum, targets NIST-standardized ML-KEM rather than pre-standardization Saber, and works within a production codebase rather than a research prototype.

\paragraph{PQNoise.}
Angel, Dowling, H\"ulsing, Schwabe, and Weber~\cite{pqnoise2022} give a generic framework for converting Noise patterns into post-quantum equivalents using KEMs, with new \texttt{ekem}/\texttt{skem} tokens replacing the DH tokens. The hybrid construction described here can be viewed as a variant of their \texttt{pqIK} pattern augmented with classical DH.

\paragraph{Hybrid-WireGuard (Lafourcade et al., 2025).}
Lafourcade, Mahmoud, and Ruhault~\cite{lafourcade2025hybrid} revisit PQ-WireGuard with formal analysis in Sapic+, fix a UKS attack (PQ-WireGuard$\star$), and propose Hybrid-WireGuard, combining WireGuard's DH secrets with the KEM secrets of PQ-WireGuard$\star$. They emphasize that MTU constraints sharply limit which KEMs fit WireGuard's handshake within the IPv6 minimum of 1280 bytes. The present work is complementary rather than overlapping: theirs proves a hybrid protocol secure, this paper implements one inside BoringTun, accepts the larger messages (relying on IP fragmentation rather than constraining the parameter set), and measures the consequences on real hardware.

\paragraph{Rosenpass.}
Rosenpass~\cite{rosenpass,rosenpass-github} turns the PSK-slot idea into a continuously running Rust daemon, refreshing post-quantum PSKs (Classic McEliece + Kyber-512) every two minutes. It is structurally similar to the PSK baseline here, with continuous operation instead of one-time injection, and shares the forward-secrecy limitation of any PSK-based approach.

\paragraph{ExpressVPN's production PSK delivery.}
Membrey and Beyel~\cite{membrey2025expressvpn} describe a production PSK-slot deployment that delivers WireGuard PSKs over ML-KEM hybrid TLS 1.3, live across ExpressVPN's infrastructure at a cost of 15--20~ms of connection establishment and no steady-state throughput penalty.

\paragraph{Revised PQ-WireGuard with reinforced KEMs.}
Concurrent work by Hashimoto, Katsumata, Niot, and Wiggers (IEEE S\&P 2026)~\cite{hashimoto2026rkem} introduces \emph{reinforced KEMs}; their Rebar construction eliminates Classic McEliece static keys on the server, cutting its public-key memory by 190--390$\times$, and represents the state of the art in purely post-quantum WireGuard design.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Design goals}

The design is guided, in rough order of priority, by: \textbf{correctness} (the hybrid must produce the same session keys at both endpoints and fail securely if either component fails), \textbf{security} (the construction must be secure as long as at least one of X25519 or ML-KEM-768 remains unbroken, and preserve session-key secrecy, perfect forward secrecy, replay resistance, and DoS resistance), \textbf{compatibility} (the PSK-slot baseline must coexist with unmodified WireGuard; the hybrid requires explicit configuration of both parties), \textbf{deployability} (no kernel modifications required), and \textbf{performance} (overhead acceptable on Raspberry-Pi-class hardware).

\subsection{The PSK-slot baseline}

The PSK-slot approach is the minimal intervention: before any WireGuard session is established, the two peers run an out-of-band ML-KEM-768 exchange. One peer generates a keypair and sends the encapsulation key to the other, who runs ML-KEM.Encaps and returns the ciphertext; both sides now hold the same 32-byte secret $K$ and configure it as their interface's \texttt{preshared\_key}. The WireGuard handshake then proceeds exactly as in the vanilla case, mixing $K$ into its HMAC-BLAKE2s key derivation chain and giving every session under that PSK a quantum-resistant symmetric layer.

The limitations are the PSK mechanism's own: the ML-KEM exchange is one-time rather than per-session, so the quantum-resistant component has no forward secrecy --- a PSK compromised after the fact exposes all traffic from sessions that used it --- and the PSK must be distributed before any session can be established. The clear advantage: zero changes to WireGuard itself.

\subsection{The hybrid handshake}

The full hybrid integrates ML-KEM-768 directly into the Noise IK handshake. The design follows the concatenate-then-KDF pattern endorsed by NIST SP 800-56C Rev.~2~\cite{nist-sp800-56c} and is structurally similar to the hybrid constructions in TLS 1.3~\cite{ietf-tls-hybrid-design}.

\paragraph{Initiator (first message).}
The initiator generates an ephemeral X25519 keypair as in standard WireGuard. It also generates a fresh ML-KEM-768 keypair for this session: encapsulation key $\mathit{ek}_i$ and decapsulation key $\mathit{dk}_i$. The 1184-byte $\mathit{ek}_i$ is appended to the initiator's first message, after the standard WireGuard fields but before the MAC fields, so that MAC1 and MAC2 cover the entire message, including the ML-KEM material. This ensures the cookie-based DoS-resistance mechanism extends to the post-quantum payload.

\paragraph{Responder (second message).}
The responder processes the X25519 portion of the handshake as in standard WireGuard. It also reads $\mathit{ek}_i$ from the first message, runs $\mathsf{ML\text{-}KEM.Encaps}(\mathit{ek}_i)$ to produce a ciphertext $\mathit{ct}$ (1088 bytes) and shared secret $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$, and appends $\mathit{ct}$ to the response message.

\paragraph{Initiator decapsulation.}
The initiator reads $\mathit{ct}$ from the response and runs $\mathsf{ML\text{-}KEM.Decaps}(\mathit{dk}_i, \mathit{ct})$, recovering $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$. Both peers now hold the same $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$.

\paragraph{Combined key derivation.}
Both parties derive the final session keys by sequentially mixing each shared secret into the Noise chaining key $\mathit{ck}$ via HMAC-BLAKE2s, following the same pattern WireGuard already uses for DH results. The four X25519 shared secrets from the Noise IK pattern ($\mathit{ss}_\mathit{x25519\_es}$, $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{x25519\_ss}$, $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{x25519\_ee}$, $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{x25519\_se}$) are mixed first, exactly as in vanilla WireGuard. Then the ML-KEM shared secret $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$ is mixed in as an additional step:
\[
\mathit{temp} = \mathsf{HMAC\text{-}BLAKE2s}(\mathit{ck}, \mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}), \quad
\mathit{ck}' = \mathsf{HMAC\text{-}BLAKE2s}(\mathit{temp}, \mathtt{0x01}).
\]
The handshake hash $h$ is also updated to include the ML-KEM material (both the encapsulation key and ciphertext), binding it to the Noise transcript. The PSK mixing step then proceeds on $\mathit{ck}'$ exactly as in standard WireGuard (using a zero PSK if none is configured). The data-transport phase is unchanged.

This sequential mixing is structurally equivalent to the concatenate-then-KDF pattern from NIST SP 800-56C: each shared secret contributes independently to the chaining key, and the final session keys depend on all components.

Two properties follow. \textbf{Per-session forward secrecy} comes from both components: the X25519 ephemeral key is fresh for each handshake, and so is the ML-KEM keypair, so $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$ is unique to each session. The construction is also \textbf{hybrid-secure}: if ML-KEM is completely broken, the session key still depends on the X25519 exchange and cannot be recovered without breaking X25519 too. The converse also holds.

\subsection{Message sizes and MTU}

Vanilla WireGuard's handshake initiation is 148 bytes and the response 92 bytes. ML-KEM-768 adds the 1184-byte encapsulation key to the initiation and the 1088-byte ciphertext to the response, giving 1332-byte and 1180-byte hybrid messages. On typical 1500-byte Ethernet paths both fit in a single UDP datagram; the initiation, however, exceeds the 1280-byte IPv6 minimum MTU, so on constrained paths it fragments at the IP layer --- which is unreliable in the wild, since some middleboxes drop fragments. The implementation deliberately accepts IP-level fragmentation rather than complicating the protocol; \cref{sec:eval_mtu} measures the consequences, and alternative strategies (application-level segmentation, PMTUD-driven parameter negotiation) are discussed as future work in \cref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{BoringTun and the technology stack}

BoringTun~\cite{boringtun} is Cloudflare's userspace WireGuard implementation, written in Rust and deployed on millions of iOS and Android devices and on thousands of Cloudflare Linux servers. It ships as a library crate (\texttt{boringtun}) and a CLI binary (\texttt{boringtun-cli}). The handshake module implements the Noise IK pattern, manages per-peer state machines, performs X25519 operations via the \texttt{x25519-dalek} crate, and uses HMAC-BLAKE2s for chaining key evolution. The transport module (ChaCha20-Poly1305) is not touched. Rust's ownership model rules out the memory-safety bugs that have historically plagued cryptographic code, and makes pulling in pure-Rust crates from the RustCrypto ecosystem straightforward, with no C FFI boundary.

ML-KEM operations use the \texttt{ml-kem} crate (version 0.3.0-rc.0) from RustCrypto, a pure-Rust, \texttt{no\_std} implementation of FIPS 203 tested against the NIST Known-Answer-Test vectors, which runs on constrained targets like the Raspberry Pi without any C FFI. Key derivation reuses BoringTun's existing HMAC-BLAKE2s extraction pattern; no separate HKDF or SHA-256 construction is introduced. The only dependency friction is an RNG version skew (\texttt{ml-kem} needs \texttt{rand\_core} 0.10, BoringTun ships 0.6), resolved with Cargo's package-renaming feature so both versions coexist.

\subsection{Handshake module modifications}

The entire intervention amounts to roughly 800 added and 5 removed lines across seven files, and about 220 of the added lines are tests. The bulk sits in the handshake module ($\sim$380 lines) and the packet parser ($\sim$160 lines of constants, message formats, and dispatch); the rate limiter, error enum, device layer, and \texttt{Cargo.toml} absorb the remaining few dozen. The session/transport module is not modified at all. All post-quantum code is conditionally compiled behind a Cargo feature flag (\texttt{pq}), which gates the \texttt{ml-kem} dependency chain as well as all PQ-related message types, state fields, and handshake methods; vanilla builds compile no additional dependencies, change no code paths, and keep an identical binary size.

\textbf{State extension.} The \texttt{HandshakeState} structure is extended to carry the minimal ML-KEM material each side needs across handshake steps. On the initiator side, only the \texttt{DecapsulationKey768} is stored, so the response can later be decapsulated; the encapsulation key is serialized into the initiation message and then dropped. On the responder side, only the received encapsulation key is stored, so encapsulation can be performed when the response is built; the resulting ciphertext is written directly into the response message rather than retained in state.

\textbf{Message construction.} When the initiator builds the initiation message, it generates a fresh ML-KEM-768 keypair and appends the 1184-byte encapsulation key after the standard WireGuard fields, mixing it into the handshake hash $h$. When the responder processes the initiation, it extracts the encapsulation key, validates its length, runs \texttt{Encaps} to produce $\mathit{ct}$ and $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$, and appends the 1088-byte $\mathit{ct}$ to the response, again mixing it into $h$.

\textbf{Session-key derivation.} At the end of the handshake, both parties mix $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$ into the chaining key via the additional HMAC-BLAKE2s step described in \cref{sec:design}, before proceeding to PSK mixing and final session key derivation. A new error variant (\texttt{MlKemDecapsulationFailed}) handles decapsulation failures gracefully, causing the handshake to abort without leaking state.

\textbf{New message types.} Two new message types are introduced: type 5 for PQ handshake initiation (1332 bytes) and type 6 for PQ handshake response (1180 bytes). The packet parser dispatches on message type and size, routing PQ messages to the PQ handshake methods. Vanilla builds reject these message types with \texttt{InvalidPacket}, ensuring graceful interoperability.

\subsection{PSK-slot baseline implementation}

The PSK-slot baseline is implemented as a separate Rust CLI binary, \texttt{pq-psk-tool}, independent of BoringTun. It uses the same \texttt{ml-kem} crate and provides three subcommands --- \texttt{keygen}, \texttt{encaps}, \texttt{decaps} --- implementing the manual three-step ML-KEM-768 exchange of \cref{sec:design}, with the encapsulation key, ciphertext, and resulting PSK carried as base64 files exchanged out-of-band. Both peers then install the derived PSK with \texttt{wg set <iface> peer <PUBKEY> preshared-key psk.b64}.

\subsection{Test harness}

The \texttt{noise::tests} module contains conditionally compiled unit tests for both handshake paths: PQ message-size validation, full PQ handshakes including keepalive, end-to-end data transport, simultaneous PQ + PSK operation, and verification that a vanilla build rejects PQ message types. A separate shell script automates operational loopback testing on macOS.

\section{Security Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

The security goals for the hybrid handshake are the same as for vanilla WireGuard, as established by Dowling and Paterson's computational proof~\cite{dowling2018wireguard} and Donenfeld and Milner's symbolic Tamarin proof~\cite{donenfeld2018formal}, and extended to the post-quantum setting by PQ-WireGuard~\cite{hulsing2021pqwireguard} and Hybrid-WireGuard~\cite{lafourcade2025hybrid}. We summarize the goals informally and give a hybrid security argument; a fully formal proof in the eCK-PFS-PSK model or in a Sapic+/Tamarin model is left as future work.

\paragraph{Goals.} \textbf{Session-key secrecy}: an adversary who does not corrupt either party's long-term or ephemeral secrets cannot distinguish the established session key from a random value. \textbf{Perfect forward secrecy}: compromise of long-term static keys does not allow decryption of past sessions; each session uses fresh ephemeral keypairs (both X25519 and ML-KEM). \textbf{Post-quantum forward secrecy}: a quantum adversary who breaks X25519 after the session cannot decrypt past traffic, since doing so also requires breaking ML-KEM. \textbf{Authenticity}: both initiator and responder verify each other's identity via X25519 static keys (classical, not post-quantum, in this design). \textbf{DoS resistance}: the WireGuard cookie mechanism is preserved; handshake-init messages with invalid MAC1 are rejected without state allocation. \textbf{Replay resistance}: the handshake transcript, extended to include the ML-KEM material, prevents replay attacks.

\paragraph{Hybrid argument.} Let $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{classical}$ denote the combined X25519 shared secrets and $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$ the ML-KEM-768 shared secret. After the classical exchange, $\mathit{ck}$ already incorporates $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{classical}$. The ML-KEM secret is then mixed in as
$\mathit{ck}' = \mathsf{HMAC\text{-}BLAKE2s}(\mathsf{HMAC\text{-}BLAKE2s}(\mathit{ck}, \mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}), \mathtt{0x01})$,
and the session keys $K$ are derived from $\mathit{ck}'$ after the PSK mixing step.

\textbf{Claim:} $K$ is computationally indistinguishable from a random key as long as either $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{classical}$ is pseudorandom (i.e., X25519 is secure) \emph{or} $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$ is pseudorandom (i.e., ML-KEM-768 is IND-CCA2 secure).

This follows from the security of HMAC-BLAKE2s as a PRF: if either input to the chaining key is uniformly random and independent of the adversary's view, the chaining-key output and the derived session keys are computationally indistinguishable from random. The sequential mixing of independent shared secrets is structurally equivalent to the concatenate-then-KDF pattern; the formal proof is a standard hybrid argument, which Giacon, Heuer, and Poettering~\cite{giacon2018combiners} give for the concatenation combiner.

In the HNDL threat model, the adversary captures the handshake transcript --- X25519 public keys and ML-KEM ciphertext included. With a quantum computer it can recover $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{classical}$ from the X25519 keys; to also recover $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$ it must solve the ML-KEM decapsulation problem, which is the IND-CCA2 security problem for ML-KEM, believed hard for both classical and quantum adversaries under the MLWE assumption.

\paragraph{Forward secrecy.} In the PSK-slot baseline the ML-KEM exchange happens once, out-of-band, and the PSK is reused across many sessions: a PSK compromised after the fact retroactively exposes all of them, even to a purely classical adversary. In the full hybrid, $(\mathit{ek}_i, \mathit{dk}_i)$ is generated fresh for every handshake and $\mathit{ct}$ encapsulates $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$ specifically for that session. Once $\mathit{dk}_i$ has been zeroed from memory (consistent with WireGuard's existing practice for ephemeral keys), no information remains that would allow recovery of $\mathit{ss}_\mathit{kem}$.

\paragraph{Classical authentication.} A limitation shared with most practical hybrid WireGuard proposals is that authentication stays classical. A quantum adversary could, in principle, compute the static X25519 private key from the static public key and impersonate either party in new sessions. However, authentication cannot be retroactively broken (the static key affects only the handshake transcript, not session data), and the HNDL threat for past sessions is mitigated by the ML-KEM component. Extending authentication to ML-DSA~\cite{nistir8547} is identified as future work in \cref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The hybrid handshake is evaluated along several axes: handshake latency, transport throughput, primitive microbenchmarks, an ML-KEM parameter-set sweep, a path-MTU sweep, an RTT$\times$loss matrix, per-peer memory footprint, and DoS asymmetry under a saturating handshake flood; \cref{sec:eval_wan} closes with an end-to-end validation over a real transcontinental internet path. All controlled measurements use three platforms across two architectures:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Apple MacBook Pro M4 (Apple Silicon):} ARMv8.5-A, 14-core, 24~GB RAM, macOS Tahoe. The development machine and high-performance reference.
    \item \textbf{Raspberry Pi 5 (Broadcom BCM2712, Cortex-A76):} ARM64, 4-core, 8~GB RAM, Raspberry Pi OS Lite (64-bit). A stand-in for resource-constrained embedded/edge deployments.
    \item \textbf{AMD Ryzen 7 5700X:} 8-core / 16-thread, AVX2, Ubuntu 26.04 inside WSL2 on Windows. Included so the implementation is not measured exclusively on ARM, and so that any ARM-specific artefact in the \texttt{x25519-dalek} backend (which lacks a NEON port) shows up against an x86 control.
\end{itemize}

Three configurations are compared: \textbf{Vanilla} (unmodified BoringTun, no PSK), \textbf{PSK-slot} (BoringTun with a WireGuard PSK derived from a one-time ML-KEM-768 exchange via \texttt{pq-psk-tool}), and \textbf{Hybrid} (modified BoringTun with the full X25519 + ML-KEM-768 hybrid handshake). The \texttt{pq} Cargo feature is a compile-time switch: when the binary is built with \texttt{--features pq}, \emph{all} tunnels go through the hybrid handshake path. Vanilla and PSK-slot numbers come from a separate build without the feature; hybrid numbers come from a PQ-enabled build. All microbenchmarks are driven by Criterion~\cite{criterion}, with per-benchmark warm-up, outlier detection, and bootstrapped 95\% confidence intervals. For the handshake benchmark, two \texttt{Tunn} instances exchange bytes in-process via function calls on the same machine, with no sockets in the loop, so the measurement reflects pure cryptographic cost. For transport throughput, \texttt{iperf3} is run in two setups (M4 loopback and M4-to-Pi cross-device over Wi-Fi); each figure is the mean of ten independent 15-second runs, reported with a 95\% confidence interval computed from the sample standard deviation under a normal approximation.

\subsection{DoS asymmetry under handshake flood}
\label{sec:eval_dos}

WireGuard's handshake messages carry two MACs: MAC1 (HMAC over the message, keyed by a hash of the responder's static public key) and MAC2 (cookie-bound, used only under signalled overload). MAC1 is the first line of defence against handshake-init floods: the rate limiter verifies it before committing to anything more expensive --- and in the hybrid the expensive path now includes an ML-KEM Encaps on top of the X25519 operations, so the asymmetry between rejecting a flood packet and processing it matters more. To measure what a responder actually spends per packet under flood, a small flooder (\texttt{boringtun/examples/handshake\_flood.rs}) emits init packets at line rate in two variants: \textbf{valid MAC1} (the packet passes MAC1 verification and proceeds into the load-dependent processing path) and \textbf{invalid MAC1} (rejected at the top of the receive path).

Each cell is a 10-second saturating flood from a single-threaded flooder against a single Pi 5 responder, with fresh keypairs on every packet so the responder cannot fast-path on session caches. That freshness is itself expensive to produce --- the flooder performs real keypair generation and MAC computation per packet --- so a single flooder core saturates at roughly 1{,}900 packets per second in vanilla mode and 1{,}650 in hybrid mode, far above BoringTun's handshake admission threshold of 100 per second. CPU time is sampled from \texttt{/proc/<pid>/stat} before and after the window and divided by the number of packets the flooder sent.

BoringTun's rate limiter admits at most 100 handshakes per second past the MAC1 check without a cookie; every further valid-MAC1 packet receives only a cookie reply, and since the flooder never completes the cookie round-trip, no MAC2-authenticated retry follows. The admitted packets do not reach ML-KEM either: the responder performs one X25519 DH to decrypt the initiator's static key, finds no matching configured peer, and drops the packet --- Encaps runs only when a response is being built for a recognized peer. The \emph{valid}-row figures are therefore not the cost of full handshake cryptography (a single X25519 DH alone costs 192.54~$\mu$s on this hardware, \cref{tab:mlkem_benchmarks}); they are the cost of MAC1 verification plus a cookie reply for the vast majority of packets, plus the rate-limited residue of single-DH work. The accounting closes: at the measured 1{,}943 packets per second, $\sim$100 admitted packets at $\sim$195~$\mu$s each plus $\sim$1{,}840 cookie replies at a few microseconds each predict $\approx$29~ms of responder CPU per second --- matching the measured 0.290~s over the 10-second vanilla window.

The security-relevant finding is stronger than the MAC1-ordering check the experiment was designed for: under a saturating flood of well-formed, valid-MAC1 initiations, the responder performed \emph{zero} ML-KEM operations, because MAC1 verification and cookie-based rate limiting together gate all post-quantum work behind a round-trip the attacker must complete. The within-mode ratios (4.8$\times$ for vanilla, 3.9$\times$ for hybrid) compare the pure MAC1 reject against this verify-plus-cookie path. The hybrid build still pays more per rejected packet in absolute terms (6.05~$\mu$s vs vanilla's 3.09~$\mu$s, a 2.0$\times$ gap), but that comes from parsing the larger 1332-byte initiation and from the MAC1 computation hashing more bytes, not from running any ML-KEM operation.

A practical reading: at the worst measured rate (23.65~$\mu$s per packet, hybrid valid), the Pi 5 spends roughly 3.9\% of one core absorbing a $\sim$1{,}650-pps flood --- well within the headroom needed to keep serving legitimate handshakes. What this experiment does not bound is an adversary who completes cookie round-trips from genuine source addresses to present valid MAC2s, thereby forcing full handshake processing (including Encaps); quantifying that MAC2-authenticated path, and handshake completion for legitimate peers under sustained flood, is left as future work.

\subsection{End-to-end validation over the public internet}
\label{sec:eval_wan}

The measurements above isolate individual costs under controlled conditions; a final check is whether the implementation simply works, end to end, on a real internet path. For this, three tunnels --- vanilla, PSK-slot (with the PSK established by a real cross-host ML-KEM-768 exchange via \texttt{pq-psk-tool}), and hybrid --- were brought up in parallel between a laptop in Romania and a cloud VM in a US-East data centre, a transcontinental path crossing the public internet. The exercise was run live, as the demonstration accompanying the thesis defense this work is based on.

Each tunnel was verified in three ways. First, the handshake of every variant was captured on the physical interface with \texttt{tcpdump}; the observed on-wire message sizes matched the expected values exactly (148/92~B for vanilla and PSK-slot, 1332/1180~B for the hybrid, cf.\ \cref{tab:size_and_throughput}), and the 1332-byte hybrid initiation traversed the path without incident. Second, application payloads (images and text) were sent through each tunnel to a receiver bound to the VM's tunnel-interface address and echoed back, with matching SHA-256 digests at both ends confirming byte-for-byte round trips through the encrypted tunnel. Third, wall-clock handshake times were indistinguishable across the three variants, as predicted by \cref{sec:eval_handshake}: over a path with an RTT of roughly 140--150~ms, the sub-millisecond ML-KEM cost vanishes entirely into network noise.

This is a deployment check rather than a benchmark, but it closes the loop the controlled experiments leave open: the hybrid handshake, with its fragmentation-prone initiation and new message types, negotiates a real consumer uplink, NAT, and a commercial cloud network without any accommodation --- the observable cost of the post-quantum upgrade on a real path is message size, not time.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Choosing between PSK-slot and hybrid}

The two approaches occupy genuinely different positions in the security/complexity tradeoff space. For operators who need post-quantum protection today with minimal disruption, the PSK-slot approach is the pragmatic choice: no WireGuard code changes, compatibility with any existing client and server, incremental rollout. Rosenpass and ExpressVPN's PSK-over-TLS deployment show it works at scale~\cite{rosenpass,membrey2025expressvpn}.

The hybrid earns its extra 800 lines through per-handshake forward secrecy: every session gets a fresh ML-KEM keypair, so key material compromised after the fact exposes nothing, whereas a single PSK compromise can expose months of traffic. It is also better aligned with where things are going --- organizations facing the 2030/2035 NIST deadlines will eventually need a WireGuard whose key exchange is quantum-resistant without depending on external key management.

\subsection{Deployment considerations}

\textbf{MTU management} is the most pressing operational concern. Paths with an effective MTU below roughly 1360 bytes fragment the hybrid initiation. The sweep in \cref{sec:eval_mtu} shows the handshake completes cleanly across MTUs from 1280 to 1500 when the path tolerates fragments, and the WAN check in \cref{sec:eval_wan} adds one real-world data point; what remains untested is the path operators actually worry about --- a middlebox that silently drops IP fragments. Prudence still says to assume such paths exist and push toward application-level segmentation or PMTUD-driven parameter selection. One thing that does \emph{not} help, despite being the obvious first reflex, is lowering the WireGuard interface MTU: that shrinks the encapsulated data packets, not the fixed-size handshake messages.

\textbf{Interoperability} is a constraint: both endpoints must run the hybrid-capable BoringTun, since a vanilla peer drops the unrecognized message types (5 and 6) at type dispatch. Some form of capability negotiation --- a reserved handshake field, or simply an out-of-band configuration flag --- would be needed for gradual rollout; an engineering problem, not a fundamental one.

\textbf{Key management} is actually simpler with the hybrid than with the PSK-slot approach: X25519 static keys are managed as before, and the ML-KEM ephemeral keys are generated and discarded within each handshake. There is nothing new to distribute or rotate.

\textbf{Performance on constrained hardware} matters for edge and IoT deployments, and the Pi 5 numbers collectively clear the bar: sub-millisecond handshake overhead (far below any WAN RTT), a fixed 6.4~KB of extra per-peer state --- roughly 6.9~MB for a thousand peers --- and $\sim$3.9\% of one core under a saturating init flood, with all ML-KEM work gated behind MAC1 verification and cookie-based rate limiting. None of these signal a meaningful deployability barrier at the Pi-class hardware tier.

\subsection{Authentication agility and future work}

Authentication in this paper remains classical (X25519 static keys), which means it is quantum-vulnerable. A future version could extend authentication to ML-DSA (FIPS 204) signatures, or use static ML-KEM keys as PQ-WireGuard and Hybrid-WireGuard do; either significantly increases key-management complexity (ML-DSA-65 public keys are 1952 bytes, against 32 for X25519). Leaving authentication classical is a deliberate choice, consistent with NIST's guidance~\cite{nistir8547} that key-agreement migration (where HNDL applies) is more urgent than signature migration (where past signatures cannot be retroactively forged).

\textbf{Rosenpass integration.} Combining Rosenpass-managed PSKs with the hybrid handshake would give layered post-quantum security; careful analysis is needed to confirm the double-layering introduces no new weaknesses.

\textbf{Formal verification.} The informal security argument should be supplemented by a formal proof; the eCK-PFS-PSK model of H\"ulsing et al.~\cite{hulsing2021pqwireguard} and the Sapic+ approach of Lafourcade et al.~\cite{lafourcade2025hybrid} provide templates.

\textbf{Parameter set agility.} The implementation pins ML-KEM-768. Operator-side selection of ML-KEM-512 or -1024 is backed by concrete numbers from \cref{sec:eval_paramsweep}: only ML-KEM-512 avoids fragmentation below the 1280-byte IPv6 minimum, ML-KEM-768 fits standard Ethernet, ML-KEM-1024 always fragments, and compute cost scales roughly linearly in security category while staying small against the X25519 baseline.

\textbf{MTU fragmentation handling.} Two alternatives merit investigation: application-level segmentation (ML-KEM material in a separate datagram, one extra round trip, no IP fragmentation) and PMTUD-driven parameter negotiation. Both add protocol complexity and would require careful design to avoid downgrade attacks --- which is also why any parameter agility should stay at the operator level rather than per-connection, preserving WireGuard's deliberate lack of cipher negotiation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented a hybrid post-quantum key exchange for WireGuard, integrating ML-KEM-768 alongside X25519 in the Noise IK handshake of BoringTun, Cloudflare's production Rust userspace WireGuard --- to our knowledge the first FIPS 203-compliant hybrid WireGuard built on a production codebase. The core idea is straightforward: the initiator sends its ML-KEM encapsulation key in the first handshake message, the responder returns the ciphertext in the second, and both shared secrets are mixed sequentially into the Noise chaining key via HMAC-BLAKE2s --- structurally the NIST SP 800-56C concatenate-then-KDF pattern, secure as long as either component remains unbroken, and, because the ML-KEM keypair is fresh per handshake, providing the post-quantum forward secrecy that the PSK-slot baseline cannot.

The central finding is how little the migration costs. The whole intervention is roughly 800 feature-gated lines, most of it confined to the handshake module, with the data plane untouched. Across a performance laptop, a constrained single-board computer, and an x86\_64 server-class control, the added computation stays under half a millisecond; throughput is unchanged; per-peer memory grows by a fixed 6.4~KB; WireGuard's flood defence carries over intact; and under injected RTT and loss the hybrid behaves like vanilla in the median, paying only longer worst-case retry tails. The one real cost is on the wire --- 2272 extra handshake bytes, pushing the initiation past the IPv6 minimum MTU --- and the measured MTU sweep plus an end-to-end deployment between Romania and a US-East cloud host show that IP-layer fragmentation carries even that cost transparently on real paths. Fragment-hostile middleboxes remain the open operational risk, with application-level segmentation as future work.

With NIST's 2030 deprecation deadline for classical key exchange approaching~\cite{nistir8547} and harvest-now-decrypt-later no longer hypothetical, post-quantum key exchange for VPNs is becoming an operational requirement rather than a research exercise. The evidence here suggests the migration need not be disruptive: for WireGuard, a minimally invasive hybrid upgrade delivers standardized post-quantum key exchange at a cost measured in bytes, not milliseconds.
